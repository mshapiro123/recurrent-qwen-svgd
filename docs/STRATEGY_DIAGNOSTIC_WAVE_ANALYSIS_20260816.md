## 1. Formal analysis one: the amplitude argmax cannot carry Trigger B

The temptation in this data is the +10.5 mean at ceiling 0.08, which numerically clears the +10 Trigger B bar. The order-statistics check kills it. The empirical per-seed scatter across the surface is σ ≈ 3.2 rows, so a two-seed cell mean carries SE ≈ 2.3. Under the null that the true surface is flat at the replicated +8 beyond the 0.02 baseline, the expected *maximum* of the three free cells is 9.9 rows, and the probability that the best cell reaches ≥ 10.5 by selection alone is **0.35**. The observed argmax is therefore fully consistent with a flat +8 surface plus picking the best of three noisy cells. Within seed 0, the +15-at-0.08 versus +8-at-0.05 difference is roughly 1.1σ under a changed-rows noise model — suggestive, unresolved. Conclusion, stated plainly: **the only Trigger-B-clearing number in this wave is an order-statistics artifact until independently replicated, and no seal-relevant eligibility follows from it.** The replicated content of the surface is real and different: the safe operating range extends to at least 0.11 (χ = 0 everywhere, floors held), and both seeds genuinely improve from 0.02 to 0.05 (+4→+8, +6→+8) — a replicated dose response over the *lower* range, worth about three rows, with divergence beyond.

The fix/regression decomposition sharpens the mechanism reading. Seed 0's regressions are flat across the entire surface (45, 42, 41, 42) while its fixes rise and then fall (49, 50, 56, 53): the non-monotonicity lives in the **fix channel**, not the collateral channel. Stronger writes do not break more correct answers anywhere in the tested range — they eventually *overshoot* on correctable ones (we have seen this overshoot literally: the `15+13→38` failure is a correction pushed past its target). This matters for design: the limiting factor at high amplitude is write *precision*, not write *safety*, which is exactly the failure mode a magnitude-adaptive, margin-aware gate could attack — a trainable lever, not an inference knob. Seed 1 is flat in both channels: amplitude-insensitive.

## 2. Formal analysis two: the depth result is the trained optimum, not a failed hypothesis

The K-sweep looks like a refutation of E3. The formalism says something more precise: **the training objective never asked for depth to pay.** The campaign minimized E_{d~lottery}[ℓ(output at depth d)] with lottery (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4) and deep supervision w_k = k/K. Composing those weights, depth 4 carries about 53 percent of the effective objective mass and depth 1 about 3 percent. This objective has (at least) two global optima: a **depth-invariant** solution — land the correction by K = 1 and hold the state fixed, scoring perfectly at every sampled depth — and a **K = 4-specialized** solution that exploits the 53 percent mass. Nothing in the objective prefers one to the other. The two seeds landed one each: seed 0 is *exactly* invariant (506 at every K, with churn between adjacent depths perfectly balanced — 6/6, 4/4, 18/18 — the trajectory moves while the objective holds it net-still), and seed 1 specialized late (+8 at K = 4, GSM8K-concentrated, 23/15). The observed "seed-by-depth interaction" is not noise or mystery; it is **solution multiplicity of an objective that is indifferent between using depth and ignoring it**, realized once each way in a 1.2M-parameter surface.

Two consequences. First, the K-sweep does not falsify latent multi-step reasoning; it shows the depth lottery trained it out (or left it optional). A fair test of depth requires an objective under which deeper is *strictly better* — depth-progressive targets, not depth-robust ones. Second, this yields a clean design principle the program should adopt as doctrine: **randomize what you want robustness to; order what you want exploited.** The depth lottery bought robustness and paid in unused depth. An amplitude lottery would buy exactly what we want from amplitude — a mechanism not brittle to its deployment ceiling — because for amplitude, invariance *is* the desirable property. Same tool, opposite appropriateness, and we now know which lever wants which.

## 3. Formal analysis three: the double dissociation

Seed 0: ∂(net)/∂γ > 0, ∂(net)/∂K = 0. Seed 1: ∂(net)/∂γ ≈ 0, ∂(net)/∂K > 0 at K = 4 only. A textbook double dissociation from two draws of the same training distribution — one seed learned a strong one-shot direction that benefits from volume, the other an iterative refinement that benefits from its final step. This is publishable physics about tiny trainable surfaces (multiple optima with distinct operational profiles), and it has one hard practical implication: **no single-seed result about a mechanism lever is trustworthy in this regime**, and any recipe that evaluates one configuration after training is sampling a lottery. That is the formal justification for the handoff's Q5 instinct, ruled below.

## 7. Plain-language summary

We checked the exciting-looking numbers with proper statistics before believing them, and one of them dissolved: the best write-strength setting appeared to clear our confirmation bar, but picking the best of several noisy measurements inflates the winner, and the math shows a one-in-three chance of seeing that number even if nothing beyond the replicated +8 is real. So the sealed exam stays sealed, and honestly so. What survives scrutiny is still valuable: writing more strongly is safe across the whole tested range, both runs genuinely improve up to a middle setting, and the thing that eventually goes wrong at high strength is overshooting corrections rather than breaking good answers — a fixable aiming problem, not a safety problem. The depth result got the most interesting reinterpretation: our training procedure rewarded models that give the same answer no matter how many thinking loops they run, so one run learned to ignore its loops entirely and the other quietly specialized in its last loop — both perfectly rational responses to what we asked for. If we ever want the loops to matter, we must train them to matter; that's now written down as a rule about when to randomize and when not to. The one question left standing is the one we've never directly tested: does the small model even contain the knowledge it's getting wrong? A cheap probe study approved today answers exactly that — it reads the model's internal states to see whether right answers are in there but unspoken, or simply not there — and its answer decides which kind of memory the next phase builds first. Paper Two now has its complete story and can be written; the exams stay sealed for the stronger system the next phase is designed to produce.
